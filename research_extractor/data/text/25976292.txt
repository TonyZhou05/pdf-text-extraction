Procedure—A phase 1 trial of ruxolitinib was performed to determine the maximum-tolerated or
recommended phase 2 dose, dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), pharmacokinetics (PK), and
pharmacodynamics (PD) in children with recurrent/refractory solid tumors (STs). Ruxolitinib was
administered twice daily (BID) in 28-day cycles at five dose levels (15, 21, 29, 39, and 50 mg/m2/
dose). PK and PD studies were performed during Cycle 1. Toxicity, preliminary efficacy, and
PK/PD were also assessed in children with relapsed/refractory hematologic malignancies (HMs).
Results—Forty-nine patients were enrolled, 28 with STs (dose escalation cohort) and 21 with
HMs. Ruxolitinib was well-tolerated with one DLT per cohort of 6 patients at dose levels (DLs)
2-5. One patient with a ST had grade 5 multi-organ failure at DL2. One patient each at DL3 and
DL4 had a grade 4 neutropenia, and one patient at DL5 had a grade 4 creatinine phosphokinase
elevation. No objective responses were observed in patients with STs. One patient with
polycythemia vera achieved a partial response and received 18 cycles of ruxolitinib. The PK of
ruxolitinib were similar to that in adults. Partial inhibition of phosphorylated JAK2, STAT5, and
S6 was observed in in vitro plasma inhibitory activity PD assay.
Conclusion—Ruxolitinib was well-tolerated in children with refractory cancer. The
recommended phase 2 dose for continuous BID oral administration is 50 mg/m2/dose. Subsequent
evaluation of ruxolitinib in combination with cytotoxic chemotherapy in children, adolescents, and
young adults with JAK-mutant leukemias is planned.

INTRODUCTION
Activation of tyrosine kinases and associated proteins commonly occurs in solid tumors,
leukemias, and myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs). Phosphorylation of Janus kinase and
signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) proteins is critical for cytokine
signaling and signal transduction in mammalian cells, and constitutive activation of JAK/
STAT and related pathway signaling has been identified in several hematologic
malignancies and some solid tumors [1,2].
While contemporary cure rates for childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) approach
85%, relapse remains the most frequent cause of treatment failure and death [3]. Outcomes
for adults with newly-diagnosed disease are substantially worse and possibly attributable to
the heterogeneous biologic subsets in adult ALL [4,5]. Consequently, identifying genomic
alterations that may be targeted with specific therapies in children, adolescents, and young
adults at the highest risk of relapse has been an area of intense focus. Over the past decade,
we have studied genomic profiles of primary leukemic blasts in association with clinical
variables to develop powerful relapse predictors and potential avenues for new therapies.
One example is the discovery of a Ph-like gene expression profile in approximately 15% of
high-risk pediatric B-ALL patients enrolled on Children's Oncology Group (COG)
AALL0232 who lack the classic BCR-ABL1 fusion gene [6,7]. Only 63% of patients with
Ph-like ALL remain in remission at 5 years vs. 86% of those without this signature,
highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic approaches [7].

Through the TARGET (Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective
Treatments) initiative sponsored by the National Cancer Institute (NCI), other novel
genomic alterations in childhood ALL have been discovered using a variety of sequencing
technologies [7-10]. For example, JAK1 and JAK2 point mutations have been detected in
children with Down Syndrome-associated and non-Down Syndrome-associated ALL
[9,11,12]. JAK point mutations are frequently accompanied by genomic rearrangements and
occasionally point mutations of CRLF2 (cytokine receptor like factor-2), which leads to
overexpression of the encoded protein, thymic stromal lymphopoietin receptor (TSLPR)
[9,13,14]. JAK mutations and TSLPR overexpression confer growth factor independence in
vitro, which can be overcome by pharmacologic JAK inhibition [9,11,12]. In addition,
primary patient ALL cells have been shown to have high basal levels of phosphorylated
JAK2 and STAT5 that can be inhibited in vitro by the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib [15].
Constitutive activation of JAK-STAT signaling has also been reported in solid tumors and
lymphoma, and decreased tumor growth with JAK inhibition has been demonstrated in vitro
and in vivo in preclinical models [16,17], as well as in an early phase clinical trial for
patients with refractory lymphoma [18]. Ruxolitinib (INCB018424) is a potent, orally
bioavailable inhibitor of the JAK family of kinases with particular efficacy against JAK1
and JAK2. In clinical trials for adults with MPNs, single-agent ruxolitinib has been well-
tolerated with reversible thrombocytopenia as its major dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) [19].
We report the results of a Children's Oncology Group (COG) phase 1 trial (ADVL1011) of
ruxolitinib with BID continuous oral dosing in children with refractory or recurrent solid
tumors (Part A) or relapsed leukemia or MPNs (Part B). Secondary aims included evaluation
of the pharmacokinetics (PK) of ruxolitinib, as well as a preliminary assessment of the
pharmacodynamic (PD) effects of ruxolitinib upon intracellular kinase signaling.
METHODS
Patient eligibility
Patients >12 months and <22 years of age with recurrent or refractory solid tumors (STs;
Part A), including lymphomas, were eligible. In addition, patients with hematologic
malignancies (HMs), including relapsed or refractory ALL or acute myeloid leukemia
(AML), and patients with chronic MPNs (polycythemia vera [PV], essential
thrombocythemia, juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia [JMML]) without known curative
therapies (Part B) were eligible to enroll at one dose level below that at which patients with
STs were enrolling. Other eligibility criteria included a Lansky or Karnofsky score ≥50,
recovery from acute toxic effects of prior therapy, and ≥7 days from prior growth factor
therapy (if short-acting or 14 days if long-acting). Patients with STs had to be ≥3 weeks
from receipt of myelosuppressive chemotherapy. Patients with HMs had to be ≥2 weeks
from prior cytotoxic therapy with the exception of maintenance-type ALL therapy for which
there was no washout period. Patients had to be >3 months from prior stem cell transplant
and without evidence of graft-versus-host disease. At least two weeks from local radiation
had to have elapsed. Patients who were on corticosteroids must have been taking a stable or
decreasing dose for 7 days prior to enrollment. Hydroxyurea use was permitted until 24
hours prior to the first dose of ruxolitinib.

Other requirements for patients with STs without known bone marrow involvement included
adequate bone marrow function (peripheral absolute neutrophil count >1,000/μL, platelet
count >100,000/μL, hemoglobin >8.0 g/dL, and transfusion independence). Patients with
STs with marrow involvement and transfusion-dependent hematologic malignancies patients
could enroll on study, but did not contribute data to the assessment of hematologic DLTs.
Other standard organ function requirements included an age-adjusted normal serum
creatinine or glomerular filtration rate (GFR) ≥70 mL/min/1.73 m2 and normal liver function
(total bilirubin ≤1.5x the institutional upper limit of normal for age, ALT ≤110 U/L, and
albumin ≥2 g/dL).
The institutional review boards of participating institutions approved the protocol. Written
informed consent from patients ≥18 years or from parents/legal guardians of children age
<18 years was obtained (and assent as appropriate) according to individual institutional
policies.
Drug administration and study design
The primary objectives of the study were to 1) estimate the maximum tolerated dose (MTD)
or recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) of ruxolitinib administered orally continuously twice
daily to children with relapsed or refractory solid tumors, 2) define and describe the
toxicities of ruxolitinib, and 3) characterize the PK of ruxolitinib in children with relapsed/
refractory malignancies or MPNs. To achieve these objectives, a rolling six dose-escalation
scheme in which the ruxolitinib dose was increased in increments of approximately 20%
was used [20]. Monitoring for ruxolitinib-related toxicity included physical examinations
(weekly during Cycle 1, then prior to subsequent cycles), serum chemistries, triglycerides
and cholesterol, albumin before each cycle, and complete blood counts (twice weekly during
Cycle 1, then weekly).
Ruxolitinib, supplied by the Incyte Corporation (Wilmington, DE) in 5 mg or 25 mg tablets,
was administered twice daily (BID) continuously in cycles of 28 days in duration. Tablets
were swallowed whole or crushed and mixed with applesauce or OraPlus (Paddock
Laboratories, Minneapolis, MN). Patients <0.6 m2 were excluded from enrollment at the
first dose level. The starting dose was 15 mg/m2/dose BID (Dose Level 1; DL1), 100% of
the equivalent maximum tolerated dose in adults [19]. Dose levels for subsequent groups of
patients were 21 mg/m2/dose BID (DL2), 29 mg/m2/dose BID (DL3), 39 mg/m2/dose BID
(DL4), and 50 mg/m2/dose BID (DL5). No intra-patient dose escalation was allowed. In the
absence of disease progression and if laboratory parameters as defined in the eligibility
section were met, each 28-day cycle could be repeated without interruption.
Toxicities were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events
version 4.0 (http://ctep.cancer.gov). Non-hematologic DLT was defined as any grade 3 or 4
non-hematological toxicity attributable to ruxolitinib with the specific exclusion of grade 3
nausea or vomiting of <3 days’ duration, transaminase elevation that returned to ≤grade 1 or
baseline prior to the next treatment cycle, grade 3 fever or infection, grade 3 electrolyte
abnormalities responsive to oral supplementation, or grade 3 fatigue ≤3 days’ duration. In
addition, any grade 2 non-hematological toxicity lasting ≥7 days that was considered
medically significant or sufficiently intolerable by patients and required treatment

Pharmacokinetic Studies
Serial blood samples (2-4 mL) for ruxolitinib concentrations were obtained Cycle 1, Day 1
prior to the dose and at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 hours following the dose. Since ruxolitinib
dosing was BID, the 2nd dose was held on the day of PK sampling. A pre-dose blood
sample was obtained in Cycle 1, Day 15 and on Day 1 of each subsequent cycle. Blood was
collected and centrifuged at 2,000 x g for 15 minutes at 4 °C, then plasma was stored at ≤
−20 °C. Ruxolitinib concentrations were measured as previously described [23].
Concentration-time data were analyzed via standard non-compartmental analysis methods
using Phoenix WinNonLin 6.0 (Pharsight Corporation; Mountain View, CA). Associations
between dose, PK variables, and toxicity were performed using SAS version 9.1 (SAS
Institute, Inc.; Cary, NC). The plots were generated using Prism 3.0 (GraphPad Software; La
Jolla, CA).
Pharmacodynamic Studies
Measurements of ex vivo target inhibition—Phosphoflow cytometry analyses were
performed to assess ruxolitinib-induced target inhibition. For the ex vivo plasma inhibitory
activity (PIA) assays, plasma from consenting Part A and Part B patients was prepared from
peripheral blood (3-5 mL) at Cycle 1, Day 1 prior to dosing (C1D1H0) and 4 hours post-
dosing (C1D1H4) and at Cycle 1, Day 15 prior to dosing (C1D15H0) and stored at −80°C
for subsequent batched analyses as described [24].
For each timepoint, 500 μL of thawed plasma was incubated with 4 × 10 6 MUTZ5 cells
(human IGH@-CRLF2 and JAK2 R683G ALL cell line) for 60 minutes at 37°C. Cells were
subsequently washed and fixed with paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with methanol for
phosphoflow cytometry analysis [15] or lysed for immunoblotting [24]. MUTZ5 cells
incubated with normal human plasma were used as basal signaling control, while cells
incubated in vitro with 1 μM ruxolitinib for 30 minutes at 37°C were used to delineate
maximal phosphorylation inhibition.
For phosphoflow cytometry analyses, MUTZ5 cells were washed, rehydrated, and stained
with antibodies against phosphorylated (p) JAK2, pSTAT5, and pS6 prior to multi-
parametric analysis as described [15]. Digital flow cytometry data were recorded and

consenting patients with HMs for JAK mutation analyses. DNA was isolated and subjected
to polymerase chain reaction amplification with primers for JAK1 (exons 13, 14, 15, 18, 19)
or JAK2 (exons 16, 20, 21), then subjected to Sanger sequencing as described [6,9].
RESULTS
Patient Characteristics
Forty-nine patients enrolled between September 2010 and August 2012. Twenty-eight
patients with STs were enrolled in the dose escalation component of the trial (Part A), and
27 were fully evaluable for toxicity. The inevaluable patient with a ST withdrew from study
prior to starting ruxolitinib. Seventeen patients with leukemia and 4 patients with MPNs
were enrolled in Part B; ten of these were evaluable for toxicity. The one Part A and eleven
Part B subjects were inevaluable due to refusal of further therapy or early disease
progression, respectively. None of these patients experienced DLTs. Patients received a
median of one cycle of therapy (range 1 – 18 cycles). Patient characteristics are summarized
in Table I and Supplemental Table I.

was discontinued, but the patient, who had a large left lung sarcoma burden with resultant

Post-mortem examination revealed changes consistent with hypotensive shock. Other Cycle
1 DLTs across the five dose levels evaluated in Part A included neutropenia (n=2) and

evaluated. One Part B patient treated at DL4 (39 mg/m2) experienced DLTs of grade 3

after discontinuing protocol therapy, which was attributed to progressive disease.
Ruxolitinib was not held in other patients, including Part A patients with ≥grade 3
cytopenias who were allowed blood product transfusions.
Response
No objective responses were observed in patients with STs. Twelve of the 37 evaluable
patients had stable disease at the end of Cycle 1. The diagnoses for the 10 evaluable Part B
patients included ALL (n=4), AML (n=2), JMML (n=3), and PV (n=1). Of these patients, 4
had stable disease at the end of first month, including 2 JMML patients. The patient with PV
had a partial response (PR) and received 18 cycles of therapy before meeting criteria for
progressive disease. One patient with relapsed JMML received 5 cycles of chemotherapy
before meeting disease progression criteria. Of note, 11 of the 21 patients with hematologic
malignancies had rapid disease progression and did not complete the first cycle of therapy.
Pharmacokinetics
Forty-two children participated in PK analyses; the results are summarized in Supplemental
Table II. Mean ± standard error (SE) concentration versus time data for each dose level is
shown in Figure 1A. Peak plasma concentrations were achieved 1 hour (range 1 – 4 hours)
after the first oral dose and decreased in a monoexponential fashion with a mean ± standard
deviation (SD) half-life of 2.3 ± 0.9 hours (h). The mean values for ruxolitinib oral plasma
clearance (Cl/F) and volume of distribution (V/F) among all patients were 14.8 ± 5.9 L/h
and 59.4 ± 29.1 L, respectively, and were independent of dose level (Supplemental Table II
and Figure 1B). Ruxolitinib PK disposition appeared linear over the dose range studied
based on dose-proportional increases in C and AUC values and observations of
max 0-∞
trends, and similar CL/F values were measured across dose levels (Supplemental Table II).
Children ≤12 years had higher body surface area (BSA)-corrected CL/F values compared
with children >12 years (14.4 ± 8.5 L/h/m2 versus 10.2 ± 3.1 L/h/m2). The variability in
BSA-corrected CL/F values was also greater for children ≤12 than in children >12 years
(Figure 1B). Interestingly, 7 of 8 CL/F values >15 L/h/m2 were found in children ≤12 years
treated at the intermediate dose levels. No differences in CL/F were observed between males
(n=26) and females (n=16) (12.1 ± 6.3 L/h/m2 versus 12.5 ±7.2 L/h/m2).

Pharmacodynamics
PIA assay results showed median peak inhibition of 44.8% for pJAK2 (range 9.4 – 72.6%),
58.9% for pSTAT5 (range 10.0 – 74.3%), and 62.3% for pS6 (range 0.9 – 85.8%) by
phosphoflow cytometric analysis at C1D1H4. Median inhibition at C1D15 steady state was
21.5% for pJAK2 (range −50.6 – 80.3%), 46.3% for pSTAT5 (range −50.6 – 80.4%), and
59.2% for pS6 (range −55.5 – 85.7%), demonstrating some loss of JAK/STAT pathway
inhibition at trough (p <0.0001 for both pJAK2 and pSTAT5), but sustained PI3K/mTOR
pathway inhibition (Figure 2A). A subset of patients demonstrated increased phosphoprotein
levels at steady state and loss of signaling inhibition (Figure 2A), suggesting signaling
upregulation during treatment (24). With the exception of increased peak pSTAT5 inhibition
at DL2 and DL5, further increases in signaling inhibition were not observed with increasing
doses of ruxolitinib (Figure 2A). Immunoblotting studies showed median pSTAT5 inhibition
of 80% (range 11 – 98%) and 51% (range −154 – 93%) at C1D1H4 (n=37 patients) and
C1D15H0 (n=32 patients), respectively (Figure 2B). Comparison of pSTAT5 phosphoflow
cytometry and immunoblotting PIA data demonstrated strong methodological correlation
(Figure 2C).
In vivo inhibition of signal transduction was also observed in Part B patients’ blood
specimens analyzed by whole blood phosphoflow cytometry (Supplemental Figure 1A).
Three B-ALL patient specimens studied in these assays demonstrated modest inhibition of
pJAK2, pSTAT5, and pS6 at steady state dosing (representative patient data shown in
Supplemental Figure 1B). However, ex vivo exposure of blood specimens to 1 uM
ruxolitinib demonstrated additional signaling inhibition, suggesting lack of complete in vivo
PD inhibition at the doses of ruxolitinib studied (Supplemental 1C). In addition, analysis of
samples from a relapsed AML patient treated at DL3 demonstrated no basal activation or
inhibition of JAK/STAT signaling proteins, but marked upregulation of PI3K and MAPK
pathway proteins (Supplemental Figure 1D).
Targeted Mutation Analysis
All Part B patients who consented for the JAK mutation analyses were assessed. No JAK1 or
JAK2 point mutations or increased TSLPR staining by flow cytometry were identified in
leukemia patients. The patient with PV had a JAK2 V617F mutation.
DISCUSSION
In summary, ruxolitinib was well-tolerated in children with recurrent or refractory
malignancies at all dose levels tested. While no objective responses were documented, 8
patients received >1 cycle of ruxolitinib. Of the 10 evaluable patients with HMs, 4 had
stable disease at the end of first month of therapy, including 2 with relapsed JMML.
Notably, 11 of the 21 patients with HMs progressed by the defined criteria during the first
month of induction, which highlights the challenges associated with enrollment of children
with acute leukemias on dose escalation studies. One PR occurred in the patient with PV,
but she discontinued ruxolitinib after Cycle 18 with documented disease progression.

Similar PK in children was observed as in healthy adults or adults with myelofibrosis [23].
PK in male and female children were similar with greater variability in younger children
possibly due to differences in P450 expression. PD analyses showed moderate JAK/STAT
signaling inhibition of pSTAT5 at all dose levels that was relatively dose-independent.
Unfortunately, there were no patients with JAK-mutant acute leukemias (either point
mutation or fusion) enrolled on the study. Therefore, our correlative data are limited and
reflective of what happens in cells that harbor wild-type JAK2. The level of phosphoprotein
inhibition that may correlate with clinical responses in patients with JAK-mutant cancers
thus remains unknown. While 50-60% pJAK2/STAT5/S6 inhibition measured by our
indirect PIA assays perhaps seems numerically inadequate, the PD data demonstrate
relatively dose-independent phosphoprotein inhibition by ruxolitinib. It is possible that such
inhibition may correlate with clinical responses in JAK-mutant leukemias; our data also
suggest that it may not be possible to achieve further phosphoprotein inhibition in vivo with
dose escalation of ruxolitinib. Interestingly, preclinical data in murine xenograft models of
JAK-mutant ALL demonstrate inhibition of leukemia proliferation with ruxolitinib
monotherapy (24) at doses that produced similar steady-state levels (approximately 500 nM)
as in patients in this phase 1 trial.
Despite the lack of a clear response to ruxolitinib in the relapsed/refractory pediatric cancer
population, it should be noted that (with the exception of patients with JAK2-mutant PV) no
patients were enrolled who would have been predicted to respond to ruxolitinib. Extensive
data indicate that 10-27% of children, adolescents, and young adults have somatic activating
point mutations in JAK family members or JAK2 fusions [6,7,9,12,15,27], but no patients
with these alterations enrolled on this trial. Furthermore, these patients have poor outcomes
with currently available therapy, raising therapeutic possibilities of incorporating JAK
inhibitors into upfront therapy, similar to the paradigm shift that has occurred with BCR-
ABL1+ ALL and imatinib [28,29]. Anecdotal data support the use of ruxolitinib in JAK-
mutated ALL, as evidenced by preliminary clinical responses of several patients with
relapsed or chemorefractory JAK2-mutant ALL treated with ruxolitinib monotherapy
(personal communication, Loh). In light of the safety and tolerability of this agent in the
current study, testing ruxolitinib with traditional chemotherapy is a rational and logical next
step in the journey to incorporate targeted therapies into our armamentarium to improve cure
rates for leukemia patients with JAK lesions or evidence of dependence on JAK/STAT
signaling.
