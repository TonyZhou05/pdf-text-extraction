Abstract
Background: Chemo-radiation (chemoRT) has improved the overall survival for locally
advanced cervical cancer (LACC) though women whose disease involves the para-aortic nodes
(PAN) experience recurrence rates and worse survival outcomes compared to those without PAN
involvement. This Phase I study determined if additional cycles of systemic chemotherapy could
be safely added to extended field chemoRT in this population of patients.
Methods: Women with LACC and documented positive PAN were eligible for treatment. All
women were treated with extended field radiation and brachytherapy and concurrent cisplatin 40
mg/m2 weekly for six weeks. Four to six weeks after completion of chemoRT, patients were
treated with four cycles of paclitaxel 135 mg/m2 and escalating doses of carboplatin (Dose Level
(DL) 1= AUC 4, DL2 = AUC 5).
Results: Eleven women were entered on study and 9 were evaluable for dose limiting toxicities
(DLT). Two women (1 in each of 2 DLs) did not complete chemoRT and so were not evaluable for
DLT. Three women completed all 10 cycles at DL 1 with no DLTs. Six women were then treated
at DL 2. For the 10 patients evaluable for response, the ORR was 60% (CR+PR). PFS and OS at
12 months were 60% and 90%, respectively. The predominant grade 3 or 4 acute toxicities were
hematologic. There were no grade 5 events.
Conclusion: Extended field chemoRT followed by paclitaxel 135mg/m2 and carboplatin AUC 5
is feasible in women with LACC and positive PAN.
Keywords
cervical cancer; para-aortic nodes; extended field radiation
INTRODUCTION
Despite the introduction of the HPV vaccine in 2006, cervical cancer continues to be a
problem in the US, with 12,820 women projected to be diagnosed in the United States in
2017, and 4,210 projected deaths from disease. With the addition of chemotherapy to
radiation (chemoRT), progress has been made in the primary therapy of women with locally
advanced cervical cancer (LACC).
Of these patients, positive para-aortic node (PAN) involvement is seen in 17%, which
represent a small but challenging cohort to treat. In addition, compared to women without
PAN involvement, these women have poor outcomes despite definitive chemoradiation, with
a high likelihood of recurrence and death from disease. Kim et al reported a compromised 5
year disease free survival rate of 42% versus 73% and a compromised distant failure free
survival rate of 47% vs 82% in PAN patients. For PAN patients treated on GOG-0125 with
extended field radiation and concomitant 5-FU and cisplatin as radiosensitization, there was
a 33% diseasefree survival and a 39% overall survival at 3 years. In RTOG 90–01 the
addition of cisplatin and 5-FU to extended field RT alone resulted in an overall reduction in
disease recurrence of 51% and improved survival outcomes (41% vs 67%) at 8 years. While

term grade 3–4 GI toxicity was similar in both arms, 13% versus 12%.
Because patients with positive PAN are at significant risk of systemic progression despite
standard chemo radiation, the administration of systemic chemotherapy after chemo
radiation may improve outcomes. The safety of such a regimen has been supported by
several Gynecologic Oncology Group (GOG) trials, albeit not in this precise population. For
example, in GOG-9804, extended-field radiation plus paclitaxel and cisplatin (during RT)
was found to be tolerable. For women with recurrent cervical cancer (who predominantly
had received prior chemo radiation), GOG-0169 and GOG-0179 established the superiority
of combination chemotherapy over cisplatin alone. Finally, GOG-0204 established paclitaxel
and cisplatin as the standard comparator arm for the subsequently treated randomized trial,
GOG-0240, which enrolled a population of women with recurrent, advanced, or metastatic
cervical cancer. GOG 240 assessed the addition of bevacizumab both to the standard
paclitaxel cisplatin combination as well as to a non-platinum based doublet (paclitaxel plus
topotecan) and at the addition of bevacizumab to both chemotherapy regimens. Data from
several studies support the use of carboplatin rather than cisplatin in combination with
paclitaxel.,
Building on these data, we evaluated the safety and efficacy of additional chemotherapy
immediately following primary chemoRT in women with newly diagnosed cervical cancer
that involved the PAN. We therefore performed a Phase I feasibility study of extended field
radiation with weekly cisplatin followed by four cycles of paclitaxel and carboplatin, in a
dose-finding design.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Patients
Women with histologically confirmed cervical cancer (squamous, adenocarcinoma, or
adenosquamous) including those FIGO clinical stages IB-IVA were eligible. All participants
underwent clinical staging as permitted by FIGO rules. An abdominal and pelvic CT scan
with intravenous contrast was required, as was radiologic assessment of the chest with either
chest CT or chest x-ray. All patients were required to undergo PET/CT, fine needle
aspiration (FNA), or surgical assessment of the para-aortic nodes for confirmation of
involvement. Patients were required to have GOG performance status of 0–2; adequate bone

All patients signed written, informed consent before study entry in compliance with

Treatment plan
Patients received extended field radiation (i.e., a continuous radiation treatment portal
encompassing pelvic nodes and para-aortic nodes), for 5 consecutive weeks. The total dose
delivered was 4500 cGy in 30 fractions. A five consecutive day parametrial boost was given
in the sixth week to boost the parametria to 5400 cGy. Six once weekly cisplatin infusions
were coadministered with extended field and parametrial boost radiation. Brachytherapy was
completed within three weeks of completion of external beam irradiation. Either LDR or
HDR was allowed based on institutional preference. All radiation was required to be
completed within 56 ± 3 days of initiation. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered 4–6
weeks after completion of chemoRT.
Radiation therapy—External beam radiation was delivered using a linear accelerator with
a photon energy of 10 MV or greater. Source-to-axis distance (SAD) of 100cm was required.
Intracavitary low dose rate (LDR) brachytherapy was delivered using 137cesium.
Intracavitary high dose rate (HDR) brachytherapy was an allowed alternative and was
delivered with 192iridium. Intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and helical
tomotherapy (TOMO) were not permitted on this study. All patients were also treated with
extended field radiation. Treatment to the extended fields was delivered using a half-beam
technique. All fields were treated each day.
Concurrent chemotherapy—Patients received cisplatin treatments as an IV infusion
over 1 hour during the radiation therapy at a dose of 40 mg/m2 (maximum dose of 70 mg)
weekly for 6 weeks. Cisplatin infusions were preferably given on Mondays. Dose reductions
for toxicity were specified.
Adjuvant chemotherapy—Four to six weeks following completion of chemoRT, patients
initiated combination chemotherapy with four cycles of paclitaxel 135 mg/m2 over 3 hours

Evaluation of toxicity, dose modifications, and assessment of response:
Toxicities throughout the study were graded according to National Cancer Institute Common
Toxicity Criteria Adverse Events version 4.0 (CTCAE v4.0). Dose limiting toxicities (DLTs)
were determined in the first cycle of adjuvant chemotherapy (after completion of chemoRT).
Dose limiting hematologic toxicities were defined as the following: dose delay > 3 weeks
due to failure to recover counts, febrile neutropenia, grade 4 neutropenia for greater than 7
days, and grade 4 thrombocytopenia or clinically significant bleeding associated with grade
3 thrombocytopenia. Dose limiting non-hematologic toxicities were defined as non-
hematologic grade 3 or 4 toxicity with the exception of grade 3–4 allergic/hypersensitivity
reaction and grade 3 anorexia, constipation, fatigue, nausea and/or vomiting lasting <48
hours, dehydration or electrolyte abnormalities resulting from nausea and vomiting lasting
<48 hours, and UTI with a normal ANC. Additional toxicity data was collected up to one
year from enrollment.
Dose modifications during adjuvant chemotherapy included delays in treatment and/or dose
reductions in chemotherapy. Chemotherapy was held if the ANC <1,500/mcl and the platelet
count <100,000/mcl for a maximum of 3 weeks. Patients with dose delays for hematologic
toxicity greater than 3 weeks were removed from study. Dose reductions for the most
common hematologic toxicities such as febrile neutropenia, ANC <500/mcl lasting >7 days
associated with grade 4 thrombocytopenia or grade 3 thrombocytopenia with bleeding,
carboplatin was reduced from AUC 5 to 4 for the first occurrence. On the second
occurrence, paclitaxel was reduced to 110 mg/m2 and G-CSF was added and following the
third occurrence, therapy was discontinued. For grade 4 uncomplicated neutropenia lasting <
7 days, there was no dose modifications. For grade 4 thrombocytopenia or grade 3
thrombocytopenia with bleeding, carboplatin was reduced from AUC 5 to 4. On the second
occurrence, paclitaxel was reduced to 110 mg/m2, and following the third occurrence,
therapy was discontinued. For grade 3 or 4 neurotoxicity, paclitaxel and carboplatin were
dose reduced and therapy was delayed until recovery to grade 1. If the neurotoxicity did not
recover to grade 1 in 21 days, therapy was discontinued.
Antitumor response was evaluated by physical examination and imaging by CT scans. CT
scans were obtained following completion of treatment and then every 6 months for 1 year.
Responses were defined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors 1.1 (RECIST 1.1).
Responses (CR and PR) required confirmation at greater than or equal to 4 weeks from
initial documentation

combination with paclitaxel given as adjuvant chemotherapy based on DLTs in the first

RESULTS
Patient characteristics
Baseline characteristics for the study patients are shown in Table 1. The majority of patients
were under 50 years old (8/11, 73%) and white (10/11, 91%). All patients but one had
squamous cell carcinoma.
Eleven patients were accrued to the study, and of these, two did not complete chemoRT and
are therefore not evaluable for DLT. Of the remaining 9 women, three were treated at DL1
and six were treated on DL2. One patient in DL1 discontinued study treatment in the
chemoRT portion after receiving three cycles of cisplatin due to a decline in performance
status (PS=3) after suffering a traumatic stroke. She did complete radiation and is included
in response assessment data. One patient in DL2 never received any study treatment and
withdrew consent for study participation shortly after enrolling. She was lost to follow up
and is not included in response assessment.
At DL1, all three patients completed four cycles of systemic chemotherapy as planned
without a DLT. At DL 2, three of six patients completed all planned cycles of systemic
therapy while one patient came off study due to a DLT after one cycle of carboplatin and
paclitaxel, one declined further treatment after two cycles, and one came off treatment after
three cycles due to thrombocytopenia that resulted in a >3 week delay. One DLT was seen
consisting of a dose delay for greater than 3 weeks due to grade 3 thrombocytopenia and
leukopenia after the first cycle of carboplatin and paclitaxel. As five patients completed the
first cycle of chemotherapy, this was the MTD. Higher dose levels were not planned as
higher doses of carboplatin and/or paclitaxel were felt not to be feasible after extended field
chemoRT.

majority of the adverse events were hematologic and grade 3. Chronic toxicities related to
treatment were assessed up to one year following study entry. There were no grade 3 or 4
chronic adverse events recorded. There were no grade 5 acute or chronic adverse events.

incidence of LACC. Women with para-aortic node positive LACC present a special

challenge with respect to treatment: the extended field radiation required for treatment is
more toxic and they are more likely to experience recurrent disease. Because of this, these
women are often specifically excluded from clinical trials designed to improve outcomes
with respect to primary therapy in LACC. The addition of chemotherapy to radiation in the
late 1990’s improved overall survival rates considerably, but a significant proportion of
women with cervical cancer still die of disease.
The concept of adjuvant or “outback” chemotherapy, with systemic chemotherapy given
following completion of chemoRT, is aimed at improving disease control and cure rates.
Introducing additional chemotherapeutics during primary radiotherapy as well as after
completion of chemo-radiation may reduce the risk of development of drug resistance and
therefore improve disease control. Duenas-Gonzalez and colleagues have published the
results of an international trial utilizing this approach. Five hundred fifteen women with
Stages IIB-IVA cervical carcinoma were randomized to standard chemo radiation using
cisplatin versus an experimental regimen of cisplatin 40 mg/m2 and gemcitabine 125 mg/m2
during radiotherapy followed by two further cycles of cisplatin 50 mg/m2 and gemcitabine
1,000 mg/m2 on Days 1 and 8, every 21 days. There was a statistically significant
improvement in progression-free survival at 3 years with the experimental treatment
compared to standard cisplatin-based chemo radiation (74.4% vs. 65%, p=0.029), which was
coupled with a 32% improvement in overall survival (p=0.0224). When rates of local and
distant failures were reported, the benefit of the more aggressive regimen was seen in a
statistically significant reduction in distant failures, 8% in the experimental arm versus 16%
in standard arm (p=0.005); the differences in the local failure rate were not statistically
significant, 11% versus 16%, respectively (p=0.097). The ongoing ANZGOG-0902/
GOG-0274/RTOG-1174 trial is also a randomized trial that is randomizing women with
LACC to receive standard chemoRT versus standard chemoRT with an additional 4 cycles of
systemic chemotherapy. However, as with previous studies, both of these studies exclude
patients who are para-aortic node positive due to toxicity concerns, specifically the lack of
phase I data in this patient population. Nevertheless, these are the women who are expected
to benefit the most from this additional post chemoRT therapy.
ANZGOG-0902/GOG-0274/RTOG-1174, a phase III randomized trial that considers a
regimen similar to the one in the current study, although notably extended field radiation is
not permitted. Patients on this trial are randomized to receive chemoRT versus chemoRT
plus adjuvant paclitaxel and carboplatin for four cycles. They are treated with cisplatin IV
for 5 weeks (not 6). Beginning 4 weeks following completion of radiation, patients are
prescribed adjuvant chemotherapy (so-called outback chemotherapy) consisting of paclitaxel
IV over 3 hours at a dose of 155 mg/m2 and carboplatin IV over 1 hour at an AUC 5, every
21 days for 4 courses. This study has completed accrual; results are eagerly anticipated.This
phase III randomized trial will establish the role (or lack thereof) of adjuvant chemotherapy
in this patient population. It will not, however, answer the question of whether this regimen
is tolerable in patients who receive extended field radiation, the subjects of the current study.
The primary concern of the adjuvant chemotherapy in the population of patients that require
extended field radiation is toxicity, especially hematologic toxicity. The extended field
exposes a greater percentage of bone marrow to radiation, thus potentially limiting a

therefore at risk for more gastro-intestinal (GI) toxicity. Thus, we anticipated that the
treatment of these patients would be limited by both hematologic and GI toxicity.,
The primary objectives of the current study were to determine the MTD and DLT of
adjuvant carboplatin and paclitaxel chemotherapy following chemoRT with extended field
radiation in women with LACC and positive para-aortic nodes, to determine feasibility of
this regimen once the MTD was determined, and to assess the toxicities of the regimen. The
study was designed with two dose levels of carboplatin given with a fixed dose of paclitaxel,
chosen based on likelihood of tolerability after extended field chemoRT. No DLTs were
observed at the first dose level and only one was observed at the second dose level. The
observed toxicities were consistent those with expected. The regimen was determined to be
tolerable. No grade 3–5 adverse events were observed at 1 year of follow-up. Data on late
(>1 year) manifestations of extended field radiation plus chemotherapy toxicity was not
collected so we are unable to comment on the long term risks of this approach. While our
current study was not designed to determine response rate, PFS, or OS as primary endpoints,
we saw evidence of efficacy in this small group of women who are at very high risk for
disease recurrence with a CR of 50% and a PR of 10% for and ORR of 60%.
Treatment of para-aortic node positive cervical cancer patients remains challenging.
Currently available treatment with extended field chemoRT has significant toxicity and is
not always completely efficacious, as we observed. The addition of adjuvant chemotherapy
can be safely tolerated as demonstrated in this study. However, this approach is not sufficient
for all patients, with an ORR of 60%. This study was designed to be a companion study to
the ANZGOG-0902/GOG-0274/RTOG-1174 study to specifically address the PA node
positive patients. A lower dose of adjuvant paclitaxel was chosen due to the expectation that
this cohort would experience greater hematologic toxicity. A more thoughtful commentary
on the efficacy of this approach in the front line setting awaits the mature data from the PA
node negative study. Studies in Stage IVb and recurrent cervical cancer have shown benefit
of the addition of novel approaches such as biological therapies in this patient population.
GOG 240 established that the addition of bevacizumab to chemotherapy doublets in Stage
IVb and recurrent disease improved overall survival. Pembrolizumab has shown activity in
PDL1 positive recurrent cervical cancer and was recently granted FDA approval. Certainly,
the benefit of these novel approaches in recurrent disease brings the hope that, if
incorporated into the front line setting, these high risk patients may benefit.even further with
higher response rates. While not the subject of this study, certainly further evaluation of the
addition of biological therapies that achieve better systemic consolidation may allow for
treatment with more tailored and smaller radiation fields or even perhaps with the omission
of radiation entirely in order to mitigate toxicity. Ultimately, continued exploration of novel
approaches to achieve better consolidation of systemic risk with novel agents with more
tolerable side effect profiles in the front line setting is needed.
