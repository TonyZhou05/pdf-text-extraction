Abstract
Purpose—Targeting aberrant DNA hypermethylation in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL)
and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) with decitabine may reverse epigenetic silencing in B-cell
malignancies.
Methods—Twenty patients were enrolled in two phase I trials to determine the minimum
effective pharmacologic dose (MEPD) of decitabine in patients with relapsed/refractory CLL
(n=16) and NHL (n=4).
Results—Patients received 1–3 cycles of decitabine. Dose limiting toxicity (DLT) was observed
in 2 of 4 CLL and 2 of 2 NHL patients receiving decitabine at 15 mg/m2/d days 1–10, consisting
of grade 3–4 thrombocytopenia and hyperbilirubinemia. Six patients with CLL received decitabine
at 10 mg/m2/d days 1–10 without DLT; however, re-expression of methylated genes or changes in
global DNA methylation were not observed. Therefore, a 5-day decitabine schedule was

INTRODUCTION
Epigenetic modifications including DNA methylation that favor transcriptionally repressed
chromatin have been identified in B-cell malignancies, including chronic lymphocytic
leukemia (CLL), mantle cell, and diffuse large B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL).
(Katzenellenbogen, et al 1999, Liu, et al 2006a, Nakatsuka, et al 2003, Raval, et al 2005,
Raval, et al 2007, Rush, et al 2004, Takahasi, et al 2004) Specifically, aberrant
hypermethylation has been described in p15, p16, DAPK1, GRM7, ID4, and TWIST2.
(Katzenellenbogen, et al 1999, Nakatsuka, et al 2003, Raval, et al 2005, Raval, et al 2007,
Rush, et al 2004, Takahasi, et al 2004, Yu, et al 2005) Furthermore, treatment of CLL and
NHL cell lines and patient-derived cells with the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitor
decitabine (5-aza-2’deoxycytidine) depletes DNMT and reverses transcriptional repression,
leading to increased expression of these methylated target genes.(Katzenellenbogen, et al
1999, Liu, et al 2006a, Raval, et al 2005, Raval, et al 2007, Rush, et al 2004)
Targeting aberrant DNA hypermethylation with low dose decitabine has provided a new
treatment strategy in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myelodysplastic syndromes
(MDS),(Blum, et al 2008, Blum, et al 2007, Issa, et al 2004, Issa, et al 2005, Kantarjian, et
al 2007) and we postulated that a similar approach may be effective in patients with
lymphoid malignancies. Therefore, we instituted two single institution phase I clinical trials
to determine the minimum effective pharmacologic dose (MEPD) of decitabine: OSU 0336,
a phase I trial of decitabine in patients with relapsed or refractory CLL; and OSU 0475, a
phase I trial of decitabine in patients with relapsed or refractory NHL.
PATIENTS AND METHODS
Patient selection
Patients with relapsed or refractory CLL by 1996 National Cancer Institute (NCI) Working
Group Criteria(Cheson, et al 1996) or diffuse large B-cell, transformed, or mantle cell NHL
by 1999 World Health Organization Criteria(Harris, et al 1999) after at least one prior
therapy were enrolled to OSU 0336 and OSU 0475, respectively. Patients with aggressive
NHL eligible for autologous stem cell transplantation were not enrolled; however, prior stem
cell transplantation was permitted. Eligibility requirements included age ≥ 18 years, Eastern
Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0–2, creatinine ≤ 2.0 mg/dL, total
bilirubin ≤ 1.5 mg/dL, and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase
(ALT) ≤ 2.0 x the upper limit of normal. For NHL patients, an absolute neutrophil count
(ANC) ≥ 1500/mm3 and a total platelet count ≥ 75,000/mm3 were also required. There were
no restrictions on pre-treatment ANC or platelet counts for patients with CLL.

Study Design
OSU 0336 and OSU 0475 were designed to independently determine the MEPD of
decitabine in patients with relapsed and refractory CLL and NHL, respectively. Dose
escalation in both trials began at a dose level of 15 mg/m2/day of decitabine administered
intravenously over 1 hour on days 1–10. Twenty-eight days defined a cycle. Six patients
were enrolled at each dose level. Dose escalation of decitabine by 5 mg/m2/day was planned
until the MEPD where 1 or fewer patients experienced dose limiting toxicity (DLT) was
reached.
MEPD was defined in both trials as the dose of decitabine that in at least 5 of 6 patients
produced a 100% reduction in promoter methylation or a 100% increase in mRNA
expression of selected genes (DAPK1 and ID4 for patients with CLL and DAPK1, p15, and
p16 for patients with NHL) compared to pre-treatment levels. DLT was defined during the
first cycle as grade 3–4 non-hematologic toxicity in any patient, grade 4 neutropenia or
thrombocytopenia lasting longer than 7 days in patients with NHL, grade 3–4 neutropenia or
thrombocytopenia on day 35 in patients with NHL, or an ANC or platelet count ≤ 75% of
pre-treatment levels by day 35 in patients with CLL.
Adverse events were graded according to NCI Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse
Events, version 3.0. Response was assessed according to 1996 NCI Working Group
Criteria(Cheson, et al 1996) for patients with CLL and according to 1999 International
Lymphoma Response Criteria(Cheson, et al 1999) for patients with NHL after every cycle.
Decitabine pharmacokinetics
Plasma concentrations of decitabine were measured on days 1 and 5 of cycle 1 using a
previously validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectroscopy (LC-MS/MS) assay.
(Liu, et al 2006b) Samples were collected immediately prior to and 5, 10, 30, 60, 65, 75, 90,
120, 150, 180, 240, and 360 minutes after decitabine for pharmacokinetic evaluations.
Calculated parameters were obtained using standard compartmental or non-compartmental
methods with WinNolin version 3.0 (Pharsight, Mountain View, CA).
Global DNA hypomethylation
Samples were first assessed for global changes in methylation to detect evidence of DNMT
inhibition as previously described.(Liu, et al 2007, Liu, et al 2006b) Samples that showed
evidence of methylation changes were then analyzed for specific promoter hypomethylation.
(Raval, et al 2005, Raval, et al 2007) Peripheral blood samples were collected immediately
prior to and post decitabine on days 3, 5, 15, and 22 of cycle 1 in patients with CLL. In
patients with NHL, core needle biopsy samples for global DNA methylation assessment
were obtained prior to decitabine treatment and on day 5 of cycle 1. Cells were purified by
CD19 selection using magnetic activated cell sorting (MACSTM, Miltenyi Biotec,
Germany), and genomic DNA was extracted from CD19-selected CLL and NHL cells using
a DNeasy kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Qiagen, Valencia CA). Samples
were processed and analyzed immediately using a validated LC-MS/MS method.(Liu, et al
2007) The global DNA methylation level was expressed as the molar ratio of 5 mdC/(5mdC

RESULTS
Patient Characteristics and dose escalation
Twenty patients with relapsed or refractory CLL (n=16) or diffuse large cell NHL (n=4)
after a median of 4.5 prior therapies (range, 2–8) were enrolled to OSU 0336 and OSU 0475,
respectively. Patient characteristics are described in Table 1. In CLL patients, the median
age was 61 (range, 32–82) and 11 patients had Rai stage 3–4 disease. Two patients were
previously transplanted and the majority of patients had adverse cytogenetics, including 3
patients with del(11q22.3), 4 patients with del(17p13.1), and 2 patients with a complex
karyotype. In patients with diffuse large cell NHL, the median age was 72 (68–75), 50% of
patients had stage IV disease, and all patients had a high risk International Prognostic Index
score.
Decitabine dose escalation in both OSU 0336 and OSU 0475 began at 15 mg/m2/day
administered 10 consecutive days in a 28 day cycle. DLT was observed in both trials at 15
mg/m2/day days 1–10, consisting of persistent thrombocytopenia < 75% of baseline on day
35 and grade 3 hyperbilirubinemia in 2 patients with CLL (Table 2A) and grade 4
neutropenia for more than 7 days in 2 patients with NHL (Table 2B). As a result, 6 patients
with CLL were then enrolled at dose level −1.0, consisting of 10 mg/m2/day of decitabine
on days 1–10. No DLTs were observed at this dose level; however, global DNA
hypomethylation was not observed (Figure 2). Therefore, both trials were amended to
explore a 5 day administration schedule starting at 15 mg/m2/day (dose level 1.1, Tables 2A
and 2B), postulating that a shorter infusion schedule would permit dose escalation to the
MEPD without DLT. With decitabine 15 mg/m2/day days 1–5, DLT was observed in 2 of 6
CLL patients consisting of grade 3 gram positive bacteremia and grade 4 febrile
neutropenia. In NHL patients treated with decitabine 15 mg/m2/day days 1–5, 2 patients
developed DLT consisting of grade 4 neutropenia and grade 4 thrombocytopenia for more
than 7 days. As a result of these toxicities, further dose escalation of decitabine was halted.
Toxicity
Patients received a median of 1 cycle of decitabine (15 patients with CLL and NHL
completed 1 cycle, 4 patients with CLL completed 2 cycles, and 1 patient with CLL
completed 3 cycles). Patients were removed from the trial due to DLT or progressive
disease. There was one treatment-related death due to cavitary pneumonia despite broad
spectrum antibiotics and anti-fungal agents.
DLTs are described in Tables 2A and 2B and grade 3–5 toxicities are summarized in Table
3. Grade 3–4 hematologic toxicities were most prevalent, consisting of neutropenia in 45%
of patients and thrombocytopenia in 55% of patients. Seven patients (6 CLL and 1 NHL)
developed febrile neutropenia, with infections consisting of Streptococcal pneumonia (n=1),
Aspergillus pneumonia (n=1), culture negative pneumonia (n=2), Staphylococcus epidermis
bacteremia (n=1), and culture negative neutropenic fever (n=2). Other grade 3–4 non-
hematologic toxicities included dyspnea (in 4 patients with pneumonia), fatigue,
hyperbilirubinemia, and elevated ammonia (in the patient who died from cavitary
pneumonia).

Patient response
In 20 patients, there were no complete or partial responses. Eight patients had stable disease
(6 CLL and 2 NHL) after 1–2 cycles of decitabine. In these patients with stable disease,
there were no clinically significant changes in lymph node size or in the absolute
lymphocyte count, with a median pre-treatment value of 4,025 cells/uL (range, 760 –61,380
cells/uL) compared to 7,300 cells/μL post-treatment (range 1,500 –76,730 cells/uL). The
remaining 12 patients progressed, including 3 patients with CLL who received 2–3 cycles of
decitabine.

Correlative studies
Expression of methylated target genes DAPK1(Raval, et al 2007) and ID4(Yu, et al 2005)
were evaluated using real-time RT-PCR in 10 patients with CLL. No significant changes
were noted in expression of either of these genes pre-treatment versus at multiple time
points following administration of decitabine (data not shown). LC-MS/MS analysis of
global DNA methylation demonstrated no significant changes in genome-wide methylation
in CD19-selected CLL cells collected pre-treatment and on days 3, 5, 15, and 22 of each
cycle (n=16 patients) or CD19-selected NHL cells from lymph node biopsies obtained pre-
treatment and on day 5 (n=1 patient, Figure 2). Even with increasing doses of decitabine (10
mg/m2 versus 15 mg/m2), DNA hypomethylation post-decitabine relative to pre-treatment
methylation levels was not observed. Therefore, analysis of individual promoter methylation
was not conducted.
DISCUSSION
The identification of hypermethylation in key regulatory genes in CLL and NHL that is
reversible with DNMT inhibition(Katzenellenbogen, et al 1999, Liu, et al 2006a, Raval, et
al 2005, Raval, et al 2007, Rush, et al 2004) justifies clinical evaluation of low dose
decitabine in patients with relapsed and refractory CLL and NHL. However, in this trial of
single agent decitabine in patients with previously treated CLL and NHL, dose-limiting
myelosuppression and infectious complications precluded escalation to a dose associated
with DNMT depletion and DNA hypomethylation. Twenty patients with either relapsed
CLL or diffuse large cell NHL received decitabine at a starting dose of 15 mg/m2/day over
10 days. At this level, prolonged myelosuppression and transaminitis were dose-limiting.
Subsequent dose reduction to 10 mg/m2/day was better tolerated in patients with CLL, with
no DLTs; however, at this dose level no hypomethylation was observed in 5 of 6 patients.
To permit further dose escalation, decitabine was then administered using a 5-day schedule.
However, despite this decrease in duration of therapy, myelosuppression and infections
precluded dose escalation of decitabine above 15 mg/m2. Therefore, we conclude that the

Similarly, in a phase I trial of decitabine in patients with relapsed or refractory solid tumors
and T-cell lymphomas, after a median of 5 prior therapies, the recommended phase II dose
of decitabine without growth factor support was 10 mg/m2/day days 1–5, with DLT
consisting of febrile neutropenia.(Stewart, et al 2009) Although reductions of 2.5–6% in
global DNA methylation were observed with 2.5–20 mg/m2/day decitabine, these reductions
were not significant (p=0.40) and did not appear to be dose-dependent, with the greatest
degree of hypomethylation occurring in the 10 mg/m2 cohort. In addition, in this same trial,
p16 mRNA expression was not significantly altered by decitabine therapy and did not
correlate with changes in global DNA methylation. One partial response was observed in a
patient with thymoma and most patients discontinued therapy due to disease progression.
(Stewart, et al 2009)
Therefore, this study and the study by Stewart et al.(Stewart, et al 2009) demonstrate that
dose escalation of decitabine in patients with CLL, NHL, and solid tumors to an optimal
clinical and biologic dose is limited by hematologic and infectious complications. These
results contrast with previous trials in patients with AML and MDS, where decitabine doses
up to 20 mg/m2/day days 1–5 or 1–10 are well tolerated and associated with meaningful
clinical responses.(Blum, et al 2008, Blum, et al 2007, Issa, et al 2004, Issa, et al 2005,
Kantarjian, et al 2007) Although a greater degree of infectious and hematologic
complications are often tolerated in patients with AML and MDS, in the previous studies,
the patients have sufficient hematologic recovery for re-treatment with decitabine within 28–
42 days using 5–10 day schedules.(Blum, et al 2008, Blum, et al 2007, Kantarjian, et al
2007) In our trial, grade 3–4 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia persisting beyond day 35 as
well as disease progression prevented patients from receiving additional cycles of decitabine
therapy. Even 5-day schedules that are associated with less toxicity and higher response
rates in patients with AML and MDS(Blum, et al 2008, Kantarjian, et al 2007) do not appear
feasible in patients with B-cell malignancies, as 2 of 2 patients with NHL and 2 of 6 patients
with CLL in our trials experienced prolonged myelosuppression and serious infections using
a 5-day schedule.
Finally, and most importantly, our correlative study results in B-cell malignancies contrast
strongly with those in AML. Our group previously reported that in relapsed or refractory
AML patients treated with 15–20 mg/m2/day decitabine on a 10-day schedule, global DNA
methylation declined to a median of 78% of baseline. This effect was associated with
significant increases in p15 and ER expression post-treatment.(Blum, et al 2007) No such
reductions in global DNA methylation or increases in selected gene mRNA expression
occurred with similar doses of decitabine in CLL and NHL patients. One possible reason for
the lack of effectiveness of decitabine in the treatment of CLL is low expression of the
hENT1 transporter responsible for the cellular uptake decitabine,(Fernandez Calotti, et al
2008, Marce, et al 2006) as compared to more proliferative diseases such as AML or mantle
cell lymphoma. In other studies, hENT1 expression and/or function have been related to
decitabine sensitivity.(Hubeek, et al 2005, Qin, et al 2009) Currently our group is exploring
cellular uptake differences between CLL and AML patients. Additionally, the lower
proliferation rate of CLL cells may limit decitabine incorporation into DNA and subsequent
hypomethylation. If this were true, it would suggest that prolonged exposure to decitabine
would be required to re-express genes. However, this study demonstrates that prolonged
decitabine therapy is not feasible due to unacceptable myelosuppression in heavily pre-
treated CLL and NHL patients. Lastly, as the pharmacokinetic data among CLL and NHL
patients appears similar to those previously reported with decitabine in patients with AML,

In conclusion, in patients with previously treated diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and CLL,
dose-limiting myelosuppression, hyperbilirubinemia, and febrile neutropenia precludes dose
escalation of decitabine to a minimum effective pharmacologic dose. However, since
hypermethylation in the promoters of key regulatory genes involved in transformation and
progression of CLL and NHL is well described,(Katzenellenbogen, et al 1999, Liu, et al
2006a, Nakatsuka, et al 2003, Raval, et al 2005, Raval, et al 2007, Rush, et al 2004,
Takahasi, et al 2004) exploration of mechanisms of why decitabine does not lead to
methylated target gene re-expression in CLL in vivo and evaluation of other novel agents
that target epigenetic silencing is warranted.
