Abstract
PURPOSE—The PARP inhibitor veliparib enhances the cytotoxicity of alkylating agents. This
phase 1 study evaluated veliparib with the bifunctional alkylator bendamustine (VB) in patients
with relapsed/refractory lymphoma, multiple myeloma, and solid malignancies, with a cohort
expansion of VB with rituximab (VBR) in patients with B-cell lymphomas.
EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN—This dose-escalation study evaluated safety, pharmacokinetics and
preliminary efficacy of veliparib (20–400 mg BID, days 1–7 of 28-day cycle) and bendamustine
(70 and 90 mg/m2 IV, days 1 and 2). A cohort expansion was conducted, which combined

Introduction
Poly ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) is a nuclear enzyme that plays a critical role in
recognizing DNA damage and facilitating repair. PARP is overexpressed in lymphoid and
solid malignancies and protects against chemotherapy-induced genotoxic stress (1–3).
Veliparib (ABT-888) is an orally bioavailable PARP inhibitor. In preclinical studies,
veliparib potentiated the antitumor activity of alkylating agents (4). Another PARP inhibitor,
olaparib, is FDA approved for ovarian cancer in the setting of BRCA mutation.
Bendamustine (B) is a bifunctional alkylating agent with single-agent activity in non-
Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and multiple myeloma (MM), and
some activity in solid malignancies (5–18). The combination of bendamustine with the anti-
CD20 monoclonal antibody rituximab (R) is highly active in patients with indolent NHL and
achieves overall response rates (ORR) of 82–94% and complete response rates (CRR) of 40–
71% (19–23). BR is also active in relapsed DLBCL, though responses are less durable (24,
25).
We hypothesized that inhibition of PARP would sensitize tumor cells to bendamustine, and
we present a phase 1b trial of veliparib plus bendamustine (VB) in patients with relapsed/
refractory lymphoma, multiple myeloma, and advanced solid malignancies, as well as a
cohort expansion of veliparib, bendamustine and rituximab (VBR) in patients with CD20+
B-cell lymphoma.

Treatment plan
Patients in the dose escalation cohort received a total of six 28-day cycles of veliparib and
bendamustine (VB). Veliparib was administered by mouth (PO) twice daily (BID) on days
1–7 of each cycle at doses ranging from 20 mg PO BID to 400 mg PO BID, and
bendamustine was administered intravenously (IV) over 30–60 minutes on days 1 and 2 of
each cycle at a dose of 70 mg/m2 or 90 mg/m2. The dose was escalated in 7 cohorts until a
dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) was observed, using a standard 3+3 dose escalation design
(Table 2). The maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was defined as the highest dose level at
which ≤ 33% of patients experienced DLT. After the MTD was established in the dose
escalation phase, patients with CD20-positive non-Hodgkin lymphoma were enrolled on the
cohort expansion and received a planned total of six 28-day cycles of veliparib (300 mg PO
BID on days 1–7), bendamustine (90 mg/m2 IV on days 1 and 2) and rituximab (375 mg/m2
IV on day 1 of each cycle, VBR). Patients received standard antiemetic therapy including
dexamethasone 12 mg IV and palonosetron 250 mcg IV on days 1 and 2, plus ondansetron 8
mg PO every 8 hours as needed for nausea.
Patients who received at least 85% of the planned treatment during cycle 1 or experienced
DLT were evaluable for DLTs. DLT was defined as any grade ≥3 non-hematologic toxicity
(except grade 3 injection site reaction, alopecia, or fatigue), grade ≥3 nausea, vomiting,
and/or diarrhea despite use of adequate/maximal intervention, grade ≥4 neutropenia lasting
≥5 days or associated with fever, or ≥4 thrombocytopenia lasting ≥5 days or associated with

Toxicity and disease assessment
Toxicity assessments included history and physical examination on all days of treatment,
and blood evaluation including complete blood counts with differential and comprehensive
metabolic panel (days 1, 3 and 5 of week 1 and day 1 of each subsequent cycle). Adverse
events were collected at all study visits and reported using the Cancer Therapy Evaluation
Program Adverse Event Reporting System (CTEP-AERS) system. All patients who received
any amount of study drug were evaluable for toxicity, and adverse events deemed unlikely,
possibly, probably, or definitively related to protocol therapy have been tabulated in the
manuscript.
Response assessments occurred after cycles 2, 4 and 6 (week 9, 17, 25), and thereafter as per
routine practice, and patients were evaluated using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid
Tumors (RECIST) guideline (version 1.1), the 2007 International Working Group Guidelines
for Lymphoma, and the International Uniform Response Criteria for Multiple Myeloma (26–
28). Only patients who had measurable disease present at baseline, received at least 1 cycle
of therapy, and had their disease re-evaluated or exhibited objective disease progression prior
to the first restaging evaluation were considered evaluable for response.
For patients with lymphoma and solid tumors, a computerized tomography (CT) scan of the
chest, abdomen and pelvis was performed at baseline and at each response assessment visit,
and a CT scan of the neck was recommended for patients with cervical involvement of
disease. Baseline positron emission tomography (PET) scan was required for patients with
aggressive lymphomas, and repeat PET scan was required 3–4 weeks after completion of
treatment for any patient with aggressive lymphoma or in any patient in whom a baseline
PET scan was positive. Baseline bone marrow (BM) biopsy was required for patients with
lymphoma and myeloma, and repeat BM biopsy was obtained to confirm complete
remission in patients with initial bone marrow involvement. For patients with multiple
myeloma, serum and urine protein electrophoresis and free light chain analysis were
performed at baseline and at each response assessment visit, and radiographic studies were
not required for response assessment.

Statistical analysis
Patients and disease characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Toxicities
and clinical responses were tabulated and summarized descriptively, and frequencies and
proportions were calculated. Progression-free survival was calculated from the start of
treatment by using the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival analyses were performed using
GraphPad Prism. Pharmacokinetic parameters were compared to historical controls using a
Wilcoxon signed-rank test. P-values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically
significant. Statistical analyses for pharmacokinetic parameters values were performed using
SPSS 23.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).
Results
Patient and toxicities
Between July 29, 2011 and October 16, 2014, 48 patients signed the informed consent and
were screened for protocol eligibility (Supplementary Figure 1). Of the 36 patients screened
and deemed eligible for the dose escalation cohort, 1 patient withdrew informed consent and
1 patient was withdrawn prior to initiation of treatment due to admission for sepsis. Patients
and disease characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Of 34 patients enrolled and treated on the
dose escalation cohort, the median age was 65 years (range, 31–86 years), and the median
number of prior therapies was 5 (range, 1–12). Ten patients had gynecologic malignancies, 8
had lymphoma, 5 had gastrointestinal malignancies, 4 had sarcoma, 2 had breast
malignancies, and 1 each had melanoma, urothelial carcinoma, carcinoid tumor,
adenocarcinoma of the maxillary sinus, and multiple myeloma.
Four patients experienced DLTs during the dose escalation phase. The first DLT occurred at
dose level 3 (veliparib 100 mg PO BID and bendamustine 90 mg/m2) in a 65-year-old
woman with recurrent platinum-refractory endometrial cancer who developed grade 4
anemia after 4 days of treatment in the context of progressive disease. This patient died of
disease progression within 3 months of discontinuing study drug. The next DLT occurred at
dose level 6 (veliparib 300 mg PO BID and bendamustine 90 mg/m2) in a 65-year-old
woman with high-grade serous ovarian cancer who developed grade 3 nausea after 3 days of
treatment with veliparib despite treatment with a combination of ondansetron,
prochlorperazine and lorazepam. This patient tolerated all 6 cycles of therapy following dose
reduction of veliparib to 200 mg BID. Two DLTs occurred at dose level 7 (veliparib 400 mg
PO BID and bendamustine 90 mg/m2, the maximum administered dose (MAD); one
occurred in an 86-year-old woman with metastatic cholangiocarcinoma who developed
grade 3 hypertension after 1 day of treatment with veliparib and was taken off study, and the
other in a 72-year-old woman with metastatic colon adenocarcinoma who developed grade 3
hyperhidrosis after 6 days of treatment with veliparib; this patient received 1 additional cycle
of therapy, but subsequently progressed during cycle 2 and was taken off study.

Of 41 patients evaluable for toxicity, the most common adverse events (AEs) occurring in

Grade 5 sepsis occurred in 1 patient and was unlikely related to therapy. Nausea occurred in

on days 1–7 who required dose reduction to 200 mg PO BID due to nausea, 2 tolerated this
treatment level and 1 later withdrew consent for multiple grade 2 toxicities, including
ongoing grade 2 nausea.
Pharmacologic studies
Pharmacokinetic data was available for a total of 32 patients, see figure 1 and table 4. The
median apparent clearance of veliparib observed in this study is lower than historical
controls (16.1 vs. 20.9 L/h; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p=0.03) (30).
Clinical responses
Data cutoff for the analysis was October 27, 2015. Clinical activity was only observed in
patients with lymphoma and MM. Eight patients with lymphoma received VB in the dose
escalation portion, and seven patients with CD20+ non-Hodgkin lymphoma (excluding
Burkitt and Burkitt-like lymphoma) received VBR for 6 cycles in the expansion cohort, with
option of additional cycles in cases of clinical benefit.
Lymphoma histologies (n=15) included FL (n=7), DLBCL/transformed FL/chronic
lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL) with Richter’s transformation (n=4), classical Hodgkin
lymphoma (n=3) and MCL (n=1). Median age was 66 (26–82), median number of prior
therapies was 3 (1–10), 7 were refractory to prior therapy, 3 received prior bendamustine, 2
received prior high dose therapy with autologous stem cell rescue, 2 received prior
allogeneic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT), and all patients with CD20+ disease
received prior rituximab. Patients with CD20+ non-Hodgkin lymphoma who received VBR
in the cohort expansion (n=7) included FL (n=5), DLBCL (n=1), and Richter’s
transformation (n=1). All patients in the cohort expansion received prior rituximab, none
received prior bendamustine, 6 received prior R-CHOP, 1 received prior allo-SCT, and 2
were refractory to prior therapy.

cohort was withdrawn for inability to swallow study drug, and 14 patients were evaluable for

Discussion
Bendamustine is a potent alkylating agent that induces extensive DNA breaks. PARP
protects against chemotherapy-induced DNA damage (4). Thus, we hypothesized that the
addition of the PARP inhibitor veliparib would potentiate bendamustine activity.
We have demonstrated that veliparib and bendamustine is safe and tolerated in patients with
lymphoma, multiple myeloma, and advanced solid malignancies. The MTD was reached at
veliparib 300 mg PO twice daily on days 1–7 and bendamustine 90 mg/m2 IV on days 1 and
2 of every 28-day cycle. We did not see additional toxicity with the addition of rituximab in
the expansion cohort, therefore we have also demonstrated that VBR is safe in patients with
B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
We encountered an unexpected rate of moderate-severe nausea in this study, and 9.8% of
patients required dose reduction or discontinuation despite maximal antiemetic support.
While it is difficult to ascertain whether increased nausea observed in this study was due to
veliparib, nausea was temporally associated with veliparib administration and moderate-
severe nausea is only observed in up to 4% of patients with bendamustine alone (20). We
suspect that the excess nausea observed in this study is related to the addition of veliparib,
and would consider more intensive antiemetic prophylaxis in future studies of veliparib in
combination with cytotoxic chemotherapies.
Apparent veliparib clearance values in the current study were slightly lower then reported.
When compared with historical reports, this difference was statistically significant (16.1 vs.
20.9 L/h, p=0.03), although the size of the difference is likely not clinically meaningful (30).
The cause of a lower apparent veliparib clearance in the current study remains unclear, but it
is possible that veliparib and bendamustine may interact in competition for enzymes
involved in first-pass metabolism (cytochrome P450 1A2) and intestinal efflux transport (P-
glycoprotein) (29, 30), and that substrate competition for these enzymes may lead to
increased bioavailability of veliparib.

The planned cohort expansion of VBR in patients with CD20+ B-cell lymphoma provides
preliminary evidence that VBR is active in patients with B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. In
this heavily pretreated population with a median of 3 prior therapies, 5/7 (71%) patients who
received VB and 6/7 (86%) of patients who received VBR achieved an objective response.
All 6 patients with FL grades 1–3a achieved CR. These data are limited by small sample size
and do not establish that the addition of veliparib improved the activity of bendamustine and
rituximab, but are encouraging given an expected CR rate of 40–71% following treatment
with BR in relapsed indolent NHL.
Responses in the cohort expansion of VBR appeared more durable than in the dose
escalation cohort of VB. This might be related to the addition of rituximab or to differences
between the patient populations, such as lack of prior bendamustine exposure or enrichment
of patients with FL grade 1–3a in the expansion cohort. All 6 patients with FL grades 1–3a
achieved CR, and were more likely to be enrolled to the expansion cohort (5/7) than the dose
escalation cohort (1/7). While all patients with CD20+ non-Hodgkin lymphoma received
prior rituximab, none enrolled in the cohort expansion received prior bendamustine and so
might be more likely to benefit from bendamustine therapy.
In conclusion, we have demonstrated safety and tolerability of VB and VBR, although we
encountered frequent moderate-severe nausea which warrants more intensive antiemetic
prophylaxis in future studies. Our pharmacokinetic analyses suggest that veliparib apparent
clearance was slightly decreased in our study, although this was likely not clinically
meaningful. We noted preliminary clinical activity of VBR in patients with B-cell
lymphoma, and these data warrant further investigation of VBR in patients with indolent B-
cell lymphoma in a phase II clinical trial.

Translational Relevance
The bifunctional alkylating agent bendamustine is highly active in patients with indolent
non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) when combined with the anti-CD20 monoclonal
antibody rituximab. We hypothesized that the PARP inhibitor veliparib might enhance the
activity of bendamustine given preclinical evidence that veliparib enhances the
cytotoxicity of alkylating agents. In this phase 1b trial of veliparib plus bendamustine
(VB) in relapsed/refractory lymphoma, multiple myeloma, and advanced solid
malignancies, and a cohort expansion of veliparib, bendamustine and rituximab (VBR) in
CD20+ B-cell lymphoma, we demonstrated that veliparib can be safely added to
bendamustine and rituximab. The MTD was veliparib 300 mg BID plus bendamustine 90
mg/m2, and dose limiting toxicities (DLT) were anemia, nausea, hypertension and
hyperhidrosis. There was preliminary activity in lymphoma, with overall response rates
(ORR) of 5/7 (71%) with VB and 6/7 (86%) with VBR. These data are promising and
warrant further investigation in a phase II trial.
