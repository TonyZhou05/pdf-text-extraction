A Phase I study of paclitaxel and continuous daily CAI in
patients with refractory solid tumors
Nilofer Azad1,*, Alyssa Perroy1, Erin Gardner2, Chiyo K. Imamura1, Cynthia Graves1, Gisele
A. Sarosy1, Lori Minasian1, Herbert Kotz1, Miranda Raggio1, William D. Figg1, and Elise C.

1 Medical Oncology Branch, Center for Cancer Research, National Cancer Institute, Bethesda,
MD USA
2 Clinical Pharmacology Program, SAIC-Frederick, NCI-Frederick, MD USA
Abstract
Background—Carboxyamido-triazole (CAI) is a calcium influx inhibitor with anti-angiogenic
and anti-invasive properties and stabilizes tumor progression in patients. We hypothesized daily
oral micronized CAI with q3 week paclitaxel would be well-tolerated and active.
Results—Twenty-nine heavily pretreated patients [median 3 (0–7)] were enrolled on five dose
levels. No additive or cumulative toxicity was observed, and grade III nonhematological toxicity
was rare. Neutropenia was the most common hematologic toxicity, seen in 79% of patients, with a
trend towards increasing grade with higher paclitaxel doses. The recommended phase II dose
defined by the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was CAI 250 mg daily and paclitaxel 200 mg/m2
q3weeks. Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed paclitaxel increases CAI trough concentration at all
dose levels by over 100% (p < 0.0001). A trend towards higher steady-state CAI trough
concentrations was found in patients with a partial response (PR; p = 0.09). Six patients had
confirmed PR (24%; 4–67 cycles, median 10); two patients had minor responses.
Patients and methods—Eligible patients with solid tumors received micronized CAI daily
(150–250 mg PO) and paclitaxel intravenously q3weeks (175–250 mg/m2), sequentially escalating
each drug. CAI preceded paclitaxel by one week to permit pharmacokinetic analysis. Patients were
assessed for toxicity, pharmacokinetics and disease outcome.
Conclusions—The MTD of the combination of CAI and paclitaxel is 250 mg daily and 200 mg/
m2 q3weeks, respectively. The combination is tolerable and has potential antitumor activity.
Keywords
carboxyamidotriazole; clinical trial; ovarian cancer; paclitaxel; pharmacokinetics
Introduction
Carboxyamido-triazole (CAI) is one of the early targeted therapies to come to clinical
trial.1,2 It inhibits stimulated calcium influx in response to ionophores, channel-activating
agents and receptor activation in endothelium and most epithelial tumor types.3–8 Calcium
mobilization is important for many cellular functions including maintenance of the
cytoskeleton, cell-cell interaction, transmembrane signal transduction and proliferation.6,9–15

In vitro treatment of malignant cells with CAI results in inhibition of selected calcium-
mediated signal transduction pathways and the inhibition of angiogenesis, invasion and
metastatic potential at CAI concentrations that are achievable both in in vivo models and in
patients.6,15–19 The role of signaling molecules and metal ions, specifically calcium, has
been previously described for normal microtubule functioning.20–22 These observations
suggest that low intracellular calcium might further stabilize microtubule polymerization.
This is further supported by the observations that ketoconazole, an inhibitor of receptor-
operated and refilling calcium channel activity23 augments the activity of paclitaxel, a
microtubule stabilizing agent, in vitro.
We thus hypothesized that CAI exposure might augment the clinical activity of paclitaxel.
We demonstrated previously an additive effect of CAI with paclitaxel in vitro with no
augmentation of toxicity in vivo, modeled in a clinical trial of a one week lead-in of CAI
(non-micronized formulation) prior to each three weekly paclitaxel dose.24 That schedule
resulted in predominantly grade 1/2 toxicity at the lower paclitaxel doses and
pharmacokinetic documentation that CAI augmented paclitaxel concentrations in a CAI
dose-dependent fashion. Micronized CAI is better tolerated than the formulations of CAI
tested in that initial clinical trial1 and we posited that daily micronized CAI would add to the
anti-tumor effects of paclitaxel with a similar or better therapeutic window. Here we test
continuous CAI administered with paclitaxel every 3 w to assess if we are able to improve
the tolerability of the combination.
Results
Patients
Twenty-nine patients with refractory or relapsed solid tumors were accrued onto five dose-
levels between July 1999–May 2003; one patient withdrew prior to therapy to pursue
surgery (Table 1). Dose levels 3–5 were expanded to at least six patients to assess for
delayed toxicities that have previously been demonstrated with CAI treatment.1,17,18 Table 2
shows the general cohort demographics. Most of the patients were heavily pretreated with a
median of three (0–7) prior treatment regimens. All of the patients were of good
performance status. Patients received a median of 5.5 cycles of treatment (mean 7; range 1–
67).
Toxicity
Toxicity was assessed using the NCI Common Toxicity Criteria, version 1.0. All treated
patients were evaluable for toxicity, tabulated by grade in Table 3. Two DLTs occurred in
the first 28 d of therapy, both in patients on the highest dose level. One patient with known
pleural effusion developed a transient grade three hypoxia that resolved within minutes of
placing the patient in an upright position. Another patient had a grade three anemia requiring
transfusion thought to be secondary to chronic disease; she was started on growth factor
support with erythropoietin after transfusion and her anemia did not recur. No DLTs within
the first 28 d were seen on other dose levels. Accordingly, the maximum tolerated dose
(MTD) was defined as CAI 250 mg once daily with paclitaxel 200 mg/m2 IV q3 w.
Neutropenia was the most common hematologic toxicity and was seen in 79% of patients,
with a trend towards increasing toxicity with higher paclitaxel dose levels. Seven of ten
patients with grade 3–4 neutropenia required growth factor support to remain on study.
Fifty-percent of patients on the highest dose level required filgastrim as compared to 0–33%
of patients on lower dose levels. There were no cases of neutropenic fever or infection.
Grade 3–4 anemia occurred in four patients (14%), two of whom were treated at the highest
dose level.

common grade 1–2 toxicities included fatigue (82%), nausea and/or vomiting (82%),

Dose reductions
CAI dose was reduced in five patients. Two patients on dose level 4 were reduced at cycles
4 and 35 for grade 4 neutropenia requiring filgrastim and grade 2 neuropathy, respectively.
Three patients on dose level 5 were reduced at cycles 4, 5 and 16 for grade 3 neuropathy2
and grade 3 anemia requiring transfusion.1 Paclitaxel was reduced in seven patients. Five of
eight patients on dose level 5 required dose reduction occurring at cycles 3–7 (median 5).
Two of six patients on dose level 4 were reduced, cycles 5 and 20, respectively. Causes of
paclitaxel dose reduction were worsening grade 2 neuropathy5 and grade 4 neutropenia.2

Discussion
We have previously demonstrated that intermittent CAI dosing with paclitaxel was tolerated,
had some clinical activity, and that there was a drug interaction wherein increasing doses of
CAI increased paclitaxel concentrations.24 We hypothesized that daily CAI administration
with every three week paclitaxel infusions would improve upon those results. We performed
assessment of safety and comparison of toxicity profiles between the two dosing regimens
and observed additional grade 3 non-hematologic toxicities with continuous CAI dosing
although no toxicity was cumulative. Seventeen grade 3 nonhematologic events occurred
among 28 patients treated with continuous daily CAI dosing, and grade 4 hematologic
toxicity occurred in six instances, with neutropenia predominating. Of note, no grade 3 or 4
thrombocytopenia occurred even at the highest paclitaxel doses. Low-grade nausea occurred
at a similar rate (84%) to that seen in the previous Phase I schedule and was tolerable with
supportive measures. Dose limiting toxicities were seen only in the highest dose level and
patients received a median of 5.5 cycles, with a median of six cycles in the two highest dose
levels, suggesting the tolerability and potential activity of the regimen. Patients received a
median of four cycles on the previously reported schedule of intermittent CAI dosing with
paclitaxel.24
The observed toxicity profile suggests that CAI-paclitaxel is well tolerated. We found that
flexible administration of filgrastim provided a buffer to paclitaxel-induced neutropenia and
enabled patients to remain on study. No patients who were started on filgrastim had
recurrent neutropenia requiring escalation of growth factor support, dose reduction of study
drugs, or cessation of therapy. Other grade 4 toxicity did not appear to increase with
increasing CAI dose or with the paclitaxel-induced increase in CAI concentrations.
CAI pharmacokinetics demonstrated that CAI steady state had not yet been reached.
Pharmacokinetic testing with second cycle values showed that with continued daily CAI
dosing, paclitaxel clearance was decreased. We hypothesize that CAI decreases paclitaxel
clearance since both are metabolized by the CYP3A4 system. There were no significant
correlations between neutropenia, response or early progression with either paclitaxel or
CAI concentrations. The trend towards higher steady-state CAI (C ) plasma
min
concentrations in patients who achieved a PR compared to those who did not might support
an argument that more CAI improves outcome. The significant increase in CAI C over
min
the paclitaxel dose range 175–250 mg/m2 may be consistent with more CAI exposure with
higher likelihood of benefit. However, this was not seen in Phase I dose escalation studies of
CAI as a single agent.1 Taken together, these results are provocative and suggest that daily
CAI combined with paclitaxel results in increased paclitaxel and CAI concentrations in
responders, which may account for the activity in those patients. Further testing of this
combination in larger cohort of patients will be necessary to elucidate if this trend is
meaningful clinically.
Our previous study using intermittent CAI to prime for paclitaxel was administered to 39
evaluable patients.24 Three partial responses (8%) and two minor responses (5%) were
documented in that trial. The current study of 27 evaluable patients yielded six PR (24%).
All PRs occurred in patients with gynecologic malignancies (five ovarian and one cervical),
as were two of the three partial responses documented in the previous dosing schedule.

Materials and Methods
Materials and pharmaceuticals
Capsules of micronized CAI were provided by the Pharmaceutical Management Branch,
Cancer Therapy Evaluation Program (PMB/CTEP), NCI, as were paclitaxel and filgrastim.
Paclitaxel was provided as a concentrated nonaqueous solution, 6 mg/mL in cremophor EL
vehicle and was diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride.
Patients
Patients were eligible for trial if they fulfilled the following criteria: histologically proven
solid tumors; ECOG performance status of 0–2; failure of prior therapy or untreated
metastatic melanoma, renal cell cancer or non-small cell lung cancer (this trial was initiated
prior to standard initial therapy of these cancers); adequate end organ function: white blood
count ≥3,000/mm,3 hematocrit ≥27% without transfusion support, platelet count ≥100,000/
mm,3 serum creatinine ≤1.5 mg/dL or measured creatinine clearance ≥45 mL/min, liver
function tests ≤2x ULN; PT and PTT ≤1.25x ULN, no therapeutic anticoagulant use and no
evidence of bleeding; no concurrent infections; no evidence of myocardial damage or
ischemia within the preceding 6 mo, no current cardiac conduction defects; no brain
metastases (computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging required for melanoma or
lung cancer patients); evaluable or measurable disease, PSA-only disease in refractory
hormone-independent prostate cancer patients; and a life expectancy of ≥3 mo. Patients
were to be at least 4 w from prior chemotherapy, hormonal, radiation or biological therapy
with 6-w requirement for patients who had received mitomycin C or carboplatin. Patients
who had progressed within 6 mo of prior paclitaxel administration were excluded as were
patients who had received CAI. All patients of child-bearing capability were required to use
contraception during and for 2 mo after therapy, and pregnant or lactating patients were
excluded. All patients gave signed informed consent before participation. This study was
approved by the National Cancer Institute Institutional Review Board.
Treatment plan
Cycle 1 was 28 d long and all subsequent cycles were 21 d. Micronized CAI was taken once
daily on an empty stomach throughout. Patients were treated with a one-week lead-in of CAI
prior to paclitaxel administration for cycle 1 only, to allow CAI to achieve steady-state
concentration. Paclitaxel was administered as a 3 h intravenous infusion beginning
approximately 2 h after CAI administration on day 8 of cycle 1, and day 1 of all subsequent
cycles. Central venous access was required for paclitaxel doses that were ≥175 mg/m2.
Premedication consisted of hydrocortisone 100 mg po at 12 h, 6 h, and immediately prior to
paclitaxel administration, with diphenylhydramine (50 mg) and cimetidine (300 mg) po or iv

Dosing levels and treatment schema are shown in Tables 1 and 2.
Patients were seen in clinic every cycle for history and physical examination and assessment
of toxicity. Noninvasive disease reassessment was done every two cycles. Dose-limiting
toxicity (DLT) was defined as grade 3 nonhematological toxicity and/or hematological
toxicity consisting of prolonged neutropenia while receiving filgrastim (>7 d with ANC
<500/mm3 or platelet count ≤25,000/mm3 for >7 d, or development of transfusion-
dependent anemia). Dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) was defined by the occurrence of one or
more events in two or more patients in a 3–6 patient cohort within the first 28 d of therapy.
The maximally tolerated dose (MTD) was defined as the dose level immediately below the
level at which DLT was documented. At least six patients were to be treated at the MTD.
Blood sampling and analysis
Venous blood samples for determination of CAI concentration were collected in heparinized
tubes immediately prior to CAI administration on cycle 1 days 7, 8 and 28, and cycle 2 day
1. Venous blood samples for quantitation of paclitaxel were collected on cycle 1 day 8 and
cycle 2 day 1, immediately prior to paclitaxel administration, and at 2, 3, 3.5, 8, 27 and 29 h
after start of the paclitaxel infusion. Paclitaxel was measured using a rapid analytical assay
employing liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry.25 CAI was
measured in plasma using a validated HPLC-UV assay.
Pharmacokinetics and statistical analysis
Non-compartmental pharmacokinetic data analysis was performed using WinNonlin v5. The
maximum (C ) and minimum (C ) plasma concentrations reported were the observed
max min
values. For paclitaxel, the area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) from time zero
(the start of infusion) to infinity (AUC ) was calculated by extrapolation by dividing C
inf last
(the last measurable drug concentration) by the rate constant of the terminal phase, as
determined from the slope of the terminal phase of the concentration-time curve using
weighted least-squares. Estimated pharmacokinetic parameters included the systemic
clearance (CL ), which was calculated as dose divided by AUC . Statistics were
obs inf
performed using NCSS (Kaysville, UT) and a p-value of <0.05 was considered significant.
Definition of response
Patients were designated complete responders (CR) if they attained complete resolution of
all disease lasting at least 4 w; partial responders (PR) had 50% or greater reduction in the
sum of the products of bidirectional measurements of index lesions, with no new lesions
identified, lasting at least 4 w. Patients with stable disease (SD) had tumor measurements
that were within 25% of on-study measurements and lasted at least 4 w without development
of new lesions. Patients with new lesions or greater than a 25% increase in summated tumor
products were designated as having progressive disease (PD).

with standard chemotherapy with hope of synergistic efficacy. Recently published trials of
CAI in renal cell and non-small cell lung cancer has not shown activity.27,28 However,
